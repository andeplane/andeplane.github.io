IClinging to a wall

A jet drags the water beside it along (entrainment). Next to a wall that water cannot be replaced, so the pressure there drops and the jet is sucked sideways until it lies on the wall. This is the Coandă effect, and it is self-reinforcing: once attached, the jet stays put.

Give a Y-shaped chamber two such walls and the jet can sit on either one indefinitely — a flip-flop, one bit of memory with no moving parts. A short puff from a control port on the attached side refills the bubble, the jet lets go, swings across and locks onto the other wall. In the bottom row of this board the power jet (violet) clings to its wall and pours out of the right-hand NOR channel; either carry jet (pink) knocks it off and into the OR channel. That is the carry-out.

IITwo jets, one collision

The top two rows use the impact element, a passive half adder. Each nozzle fires along the far roof of a diamond-shaped chamber. A lone jet hugs that roof and leaves through a side channel: that is the sum (A XOR B). When both fire, they meet head-on beneath the apex, their sideways momenta cancel and the merged jet plunges down the middle: the carry (A AND B).

A full adder is two half adders and an OR: the first adds A and B, the second adds the carry from the bit to the right, and the OR merges their carries. Four of them in a row make a ripple-carry adder, and you can watch the carry travel leftwards, one chamber at a time.

IIIWhat is real here

The water is a D2Q9 lattice Boltzmann simulation running in WebGL2: about 70 000 wet cells, stepped thousands of times a second, with half-way bounce-back walls, Smagorinsky sub-grid viscosity and jets at a Reynolds number of a few hundred. Coloured dye is carried along by the computed flow. Nothing is animated by hand.

Each chamber is its own fluid tile. The glowing pipes between them are the wiring: a probe measures how much water leaves through each output channel, and once the reading is clear and steady it opens the matching valve on the next chamber. Every bit you see is decided by where the simulated water went — the same pass-the-jet-along wiring a real fluidic circuit does with tubing and amplifiers.

One honest wrinkle: at this Reynolds number a big eddy left over from the previous answer can outlive the jet that spun it up and steer the next jet wrong. So before a collision chamber computes, it is purged — both jets fire for a moment, the collision sweeps the chamber clean, then the real inputs are applied. You can see it as a brief double-jet flash.

IVComputers made of air and water

Fluid amplification was announced in 1960 by Billy Horton, Romald Bowles and Raymond Warren at the US Army's Diamond Ordnance Fuze Laboratories (later Harry Diamond Laboratories), and the wall-attachment flip-flop became its workhorse. Through the 1960s fluidics was a serious contender wherever electronics struggled: radiation, heat, vibration and explosive atmospheres. Sperry Rand's FLODAC (1964) was a small demonstration computer built from air-driven NOR gates, and fluidic controllers went on to run machine tools and fly in jet engines.

They lost to the transistor on speed — a fluidic gate switches in about a millisecond, set by how fast the fluid can cross a chamber — but the idea never died: microfluidic logic now computes inside lab-on-a-chip devices, with droplets and jets instead of electrons.
